Chuseok, Korea's harvest festival on the 15th day of the eighth lunar month, has one signature food: **songpyeon**, a small half-moon rice cake filled with sesame seeds, beans, red beans or chestnuts and steamed over a bed of pine needles. Around it sits the holiday spread — **jeon** (pan-fried, egg-battered meat, vegetables or seafood), **japchae** (glass noodles stir-fried with vegetables), **galbijjim** (braised short ribs), autumn fruit, and **hangwa** (traditional sweets). On Chuseok morning, families offer rice, rice wine and songpyeon from the new harvest at **charye**, the ancestral memorial service, and then eat together. If you are in Korea during the holiday, traditional markets, rice cake shops and Korean restaurants sell these dishes. (Chuseok 2026 fell on September 25; Chuseok 2027 is on September 15 — see our [Chuseok explainer](/en/posts/chuseok-2026-korean-thanksgiving/) for dates.)

## Songpyeon: the rice cake that defines the holiday

**Songpyeon (송편)** is written with the character for pine (松, *song*), and it is steamed on a layer of pine needles. The needles give the rice cakes their pine scent — there is an old Korean saying that the taste of autumn comes from songpyeon, and the taste of songpyeon comes from the smell of pine.

The dough is non-glutinous rice flour kneaded with hot water, shaped by hand around a filling and then steamed. Fillings include **sesame seeds, beans, red beans, chestnuts and jujubes**; for Chuseok they are traditionally made from the newly harvested crop. Some versions work other ingredients into the cake itself, such as mugwort (*ssuk-songpyeon*) or pine bark (*songgi-songpyeon*).

Families traditionally make songpyeon together on the eve of Chuseok. An old saying holds that whoever shapes beautiful songpyeon will have a beautiful baby.

## The charye table: what goes on it

**Charye (차례)** is the memorial service for ancestors held early on Chuseok morning. Unlike Seollal (Lunar New Year), when white rice-cake soup is offered, the Chuseok table centres on the new harvest: rice cooked from new rice, rice wine brewed from it, and songpyeon made from new grain. After the rite, the whole family shares the food that was offered, a custom called *eumbok* (음복).

There is no single correct table. The dishes and how they are arranged differ by region and by family, so two Korean households can set a visibly different charye table for the same holiday. If you are invited into a Korean home for Chuseok, this rite usually comes before the family meal.

## Jeon, japchae, and the rest of the spread

- **Jeon (전)** — slices of meat, fish or vegetables dipped in flour and egg batter and lightly pan-fried.
- **Japchae (잡채)** — glass noodles stir-fried with assorted vegetables.
- **Galbijjim (갈비찜)** — braised short ribs seasoned with a sweet soy-based sauce, one of the main dishes of the holiday.
- **Toranguk (토란국)** — taro soup, one of the traditional seasonal foods of Chuseok.
- **Hangwa (한과)** — traditional Korean sweets and cookies made with honey and rice syrup. *Yakgwa*, a honey-and-ginger cookie, is also widely sold for the holiday.
- **Sikhye (식혜)** — a sweet rice drink often served at the end of the meal.
- **Autumn fruit** — persimmons, chestnuts, jujubes, walnuts, ginkgo nuts and quince are the traditional Chuseok fruits and nuts; apples and pears were added in more recent times.

## Where to try Chuseok food as a visitor

| Where | What you'll find |
|---|---|
| Traditional markets | Songpyeon, jeon and other holiday foods |
| Rice cake shops (*tteokjip*, 떡집) | Fresh songpyeon and other rice cakes |
| Korean restaurants | Jeon, japchae and other holiday dishes |
| A Korean home, if invited | Charye and the family meal that follows — not something you can buy |

Plan around closures. According to the Korea Tourism Organization, many department stores, shopping malls and restaurants close on Chuseok Day or for part of the holiday, while convenience stores and shops near tourist attractions often stay open. Buying songpyeon a day or two before Chuseok Day is the safe option. For what else closes and stays open, see our [Chuseok explainer](/en/posts/chuseok-2026-korean-thanksgiving/).

::faq
Q: What is songpyeon and why is it steamed on pine needles?
A: Songpyeon is a small half-moon rice cake made from rice flour and filled with sesame seeds, beans, red beans or chestnuts. It is steamed on a bed of pine needles, which give it its pine scent — the name itself contains the character for pine.

Q: Do I need to be invited to a Korean home to try Chuseok food?
A: No. Traditional markets, rice cake shops and Korean restaurants sell songpyeon, jeon, japchae and other holiday dishes. Only the charye rite itself happens at home.

Q: What is charye and what goes on the table?
A: Charye is the memorial service for ancestors held on Chuseok morning. The Chuseok table centres on the new harvest — rice, rice wine and songpyeon — and the exact dishes and layout differ by region and family. Afterwards the family shares the food.

Q: Is Chuseok food spicy?
A: The core dishes described here — songpyeon, jeon, japchae and galbijjim — are not chili-based dishes, so they are a gentle introduction for a first-time visitor.

Q: Will restaurants be open during the holiday?
A: Many restaurants close on Chuseok Day or for part of the holiday, while convenience stores and shops near tourist attractions often stay open. Have a backup plan for meals on Chuseok Day itself.
