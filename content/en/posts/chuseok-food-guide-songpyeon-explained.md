Chuseok, Korea's harvest festival running **September 24–26, 2026**, has one signature food: **songpyeon**, a half-moon-shaped rice cake steamed over a bed of pine needles and filled with sesame, chestnut, or sweetened red bean. Around it sits a full holiday spread — **jeon** (pan-fried savory pancakes), **japchae** (glass noodles with vegetables), fresh autumn fruit, and **hangwa** (traditional rice-and-honey sweets) — most of it prepared for **charye**, the ancestral memorial rite families hold on the morning of the holiday. None of it requires a restaurant reservation or a ticket; if you're in Korea that week, department store food halls, traditional markets, and convenience stores all sell holiday versions of these dishes.

## Songpyeon: the rice cake that defines the holiday

**Songpyeon (송편)** takes its name from *song*, meaning pine tree, because the rice cakes are steamed on a layer of pine needles rather than in a plain steamer basket. The needles do real work, not just decoration: they keep the cakes from sticking to each other and to the steamer, add a faint resinous fragrance, and were traditionally believed to help the rice cakes stay fresher longer.

The dough is short-grain rice flour, hand-shaped into small half-moons, then filled before folding — common fillings are **toasted sesame seeds with sugar, sweetened red bean, and chestnut**. Family members often make songpyeon together the night before Chuseok, and there's a well-known saying that whoever shapes a particularly pretty songpyeon will have a beautiful daughter or a good marriage — a piece of folk superstition more than a real belief, but one that comes up constantly around the mixing bowl.

## The charye table: why the food is arranged so precisely

**Charye (차례)** is the ancestral memorial rite held on Chuseok morning, when families set a ritual table for deceased relatives before eating together. The table includes freshly harvested rice, songpyeon, seasonal fruit — pear, persimmon, jujube — along with jeon, japchae, grilled fish, and rice wine.

Placement isn't casual. Rules passed down for generations dictate where each dish goes — red fruits east, white fruits west; fish facing a certain direction — and the exact layout varies by region and by family lineage, so two Korean households can set a visibly different table for the same holiday. If you're invited into a Korean home for Chuseok, this table is usually the first thing you'll see, before the meal itself begins.

## Jeon, japchae, and the rest of the spread

- **Jeon (전)** — vegetables, meat, or seafood dipped in a light egg batter and pan-fried. Historically a luxury dish reserved for special occasions because making a full batch is genuinely labor-intensive, which is part of why it's still a holiday-specific food rather than an everyday one.
- **Japchae (잡채)** — stir-fried glass noodles with vegetables and often beef, its name literally translating to "mixed vegetables." The colorful mix of ingredients is meant to symbolize a well-balanced, harmonious life, which is why it shows up at celebrations beyond Chuseok too.
- **Hangwa (한과)** — traditional confections made from rice flour, honey, and fruit or roots, often shaped and colored elaborately. These keep well, which makes them a common Chuseok gift alongside fresh fruit.
- **Autumn fruit** — pear, persimmon, jujube (dried Korean date), and Korean melon are all in season around the holiday and appear both on the charye table and in gift boxes.

## Where to try Chuseok food as a visitor

| Where | What you'll find |
|---|---|
| Department store food halls (Lotte, Shinsegae, Hyundai) | Pre-made songpyeon sets, hangwa gift boxes, jeon platters — no cooking required |
| Traditional markets (Gwangjang, Namdaemun) | Fresh songpyeon by the piece, jeon made to order, cheaper than department stores |
| Convenience stores (CU, GS25, 7-Eleven) | Individually wrapped songpyeon and simple jeon snacks, available even during the holiday when many small restaurants close |
| A Korean home, if invited | The full charye spread, plus the family meal that follows — the most authentic version, but not something you can buy |

Most small, independently owned restaurants close for at least part of the Chuseok holiday so staff can travel home, while large chains and 24-hour convenience stores generally stay open — worth knowing if you're planning meals in Korea that week. For the full list of what shuts down and what stays open during the holiday itself, see our [Chuseok 2026 explainer](/en/posts/chuseok-2026-korean-thanksgiving/).

If cooking your own songpyeon sounds appealing, pre-portioned rice cake kits are sold at most large grocery chains in the two weeks before the holiday — an easy way to try the tradition without sourcing rice flour and fillings separately.

## Regional and modern variations

Traditional songpyeon fillings vary by region — some areas favor mugwort or pumpkin worked into the dough itself for color and flavor, rather than plain white rice cakes. In recent years, bakeries and cafés have also started selling modern spins: songpyeon-shaped cookies, chestnut mousse cakes, and hangwa-inspired pastries aimed at younger buyers who want the holiday flavor without making rice cakes from scratch. None of this replaces the traditional spread at a family table, but it's common to see both versions side by side in a single grocery store display during Chuseok week.

Q: What is songpyeon and why is it steamed on pine needles?
A: Songpyeon is a half-moon-shaped rice cake filled with sesame, red bean, or chestnut. It's steamed on a bed of pine needles, which keep the cakes from sticking together, add a faint pine fragrance, and were traditionally believed to help the cakes stay fresh longer.

Q: Do I need to be invited to a Korean home to try real Chuseok food?
A: No. Department store food halls and traditional markets sell pre-made songpyeon, jeon, and hangwa during Chuseok week, so you can try the holiday dishes without attending a family gathering.

Q: What is charye and why does the food table look so specific?
A: Charye is the ancestral memorial rite held on Chuseok morning. Families arrange rice, songpyeon, fruit, and other dishes on a table following placement rules passed down for generations — the exact layout varies by region and family.

Q: Is Chuseok food very spicy or unusual for a first-time visitor?
A: No. Most Chuseok dishes — rice cakes, pan-fried jeon, noodle-based japchae — are mild and approachable, closer to comfort food than the spicier dishes Korean cuisine is sometimes known for.

Q: Will restaurants be open to eat Chuseok food during the actual holiday?
A: Many small, independently owned restaurants close so staff can travel, but large chains and convenience stores generally stay open, and department store food halls typically operate on a modified holiday schedule.

Q: What's a good Chuseok food gift to bring if I'm invited to someone's home?
A: A boxed set of hangwa (traditional sweets) or fresh seasonal fruit like pears or persimmons are both standard, easy-to-find gifts sold specifically for the holiday at any grocery store or department store during Chuseok week.
