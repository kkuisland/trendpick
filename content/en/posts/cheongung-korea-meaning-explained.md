**Cheongung (천궁)** is one Korean word that points to two completely different things, which is why it confuses non-Korean readers who see it trending. Most of the time in news headlines it means the **KM-SAM surface-to-air missile system**, written 天弓 in Chinese characters and meaning roughly "heavenly bow" — a South Korean-built air defence platform that has become one of the country's biggest defence exports. In food, pharmacy and traditional-medicine contexts it means 川芎, the dried rhizome of *Cnidium officinale*, a bitter aromatic root used in Korean herbal formulas and some soups. The Hangul is identical; only the context tells you which one is meant.

## The three meanings of Cheongung (천궁), side by side

Korean writes both words as 천궁 because Hangul is phonetic — it records sound, not the Chinese characters underneath. Native readers disambiguate instantly from context. Here is the shortcut.

| Written as | Literal meaning | What it actually refers to | Where you'll see it |
|---|---|---|---|
| 천궁 (天弓) | "heavenly bow" | KM-SAM medium-range air defence missile system | Defence news, export contracts, military parades |
| 천궁 (川芎) | "river xiong" (a plant name) | Dried root used in Korean/Chinese herbal medicine | Pharmacies, herbal markets, soup recipes, tea |
| 천궁 (天宮) | "heavenly palace" | A poetic/mythological term; also used in building and business names | Fiction, temple architecture, shop signs |

Pronunciation is the same for all three: roughly **chun-goong**, with a soft "ch" and a short "oo".

## Meaning 1: Cheongung the air defence system (KM-SAM)

This is the meaning that puts 천궁 into international headlines.

Cheongung is a **medium-range surface-to-air missile system** developed in South Korea to intercept aircraft and, in its later version, incoming ballistic missiles. It sits in the middle layer of the country's layered air defence concept, above short-range guns and shoulder-fired missiles and below longer-range systems.

Key things a general reader should understand:

- **Cheongung-II** (also written M-SAM Block-II) is the ballistic-missile-capable upgrade, and it is the version most often discussed in export deals.
- The system is a **domestic Korean product**, not a licensed foreign design, which is exactly why Korean media treat each contract as a national story.
- Development involved the state Agency for Defense Development together with Korean industry primes, with **LIG Nex1** commonly named as the lead and **Hanwha** companies associated with the radar and launcher vehicles [confirm before you cite].
- A battery combines a multi-function radar, a command vehicle and vertically launched missile canisters mounted on trucks.

### Why it keeps trending

South Korea has spent the last several years turning defence manufacturing into a serious export sector, and Cheongung is the flagship of the air-defence side of that push. Reported customers include **the United Arab Emirates, Saudi Arabia and Iraq**, with contract values reported in the multi-billion-dollar range [confirm before you cite]. Whenever a new order, delivery milestone or follow-on contract appears, 천궁 spikes in Korean search rankings and English-language defence outlets pick it up a day or two later.

For a visitor, the practical relevance is small but not zero: Cheongung launchers appear at **Armed Forces Day parades** and at defence trade shows such as ADEX, held near Seoul in odd-numbered years [confirm dates before you plan around it].

## Meaning 2: Cheongung the herb (川芎)

Walk into a traditional pharmacy in Korea and ask for 천궁 and nobody will think about missiles.

Cheongung here is the **dried, sliced rhizome of *Cnidium officinale***, a plant in the carrot and parsley family. It is strongly aromatic, slightly bitter, and warming in the vocabulary of Korean traditional medicine (hanbang, 한방).

Where it shows up:

- **Samul-tang (사물탕)** — the classic "four substance decoction", combining cheongung with danggwi (당귀, Korean angelica), baekjakyak (백작약, white peony root) and sukjihwang (숙지황, prepared rehmannia). It is prescribed in traditional practice as a blood tonic, historically associated with women's health.
- **Ssanghwa-tang (쌍화탕)** — a bitter herbal tonic sold in small brown bottles at almost every Korean pharmacy, often taken when you feel a cold coming on. Cheongung is one of its components.
- **Cooking** — a slice or two goes into long-simmered broths and braises, partly for aroma and partly to cut the heaviness of meat. You will not taste it as a distinct flavour; it works as background.

None of this is medical advice. Herbal formulas interact with medication, and some of these ingredients are not recommended in pregnancy — talk to a doctor rather than a souvenir shop.

### Where to see it in person

The two obvious places are Korea's historic herbal medicine markets:

| Market | City | What it is |
|---|---|---|
| Seoul Yangnyeongsi (서울약령시) | Jegi-dong, Dongdaemun-gu, Seoul | Dense strip of herb wholesalers, clinics and a free medicine museum |
| Daegu Yangnyeongsi (대구약령시) | Central Daegu | Older market with a herbal medicine culture museum and an autumn festival |

Both are working markets, not tourist attractions, so mornings on weekdays are busiest and most interesting. Opening hours vary by shop and many close on Sundays [confirm before you go]. Sellers usually do not speak English; pointing and a translation app work fine for buying dried herbs, though anything intended as a remedy should go through a licensed clinic.

## How to tell which Cheongung a headline means

A quick sorting method when you hit 천궁 in a Korean article or a translated feed:

1. **Look for company names.** LIG Nex1, Hanwha, ADD, or a country name in the same sentence means the missile system.
2. **Look for 한약 (hanyak, herbal medicine), 탕 (tang, decoction) or 약재 (yakjae, medicinal ingredient).** That's the herb.
3. **Look for -II or 블록 (Block).** Only the missile system has versions.
4. **Check the section.** Korean portals file the missile under 정치/사회 (politics/society) or 산업 (industry); the herb lands under 건강 (health) or 라이프 (lifestyle).

Machine translation frequently gets this wrong and will render the herb as "Cheongung missile" or the missile as "cnidium". If a translated sentence sounds absurd, this ambiguity is usually why.

## What this tells you about Korean words in general

Cheongung is a good example of a wider issue for anyone reading about Korea in translation. Korean has a large Sino-Korean vocabulary, and because Hangul dropped the Chinese characters from everyday writing, **homophones are everywhere**. Newspapers occasionally add the hanja in brackets — 천궁(天弓) — precisely to prevent this confusion.

Practical takeaway: when you search a Korean term and get contradictory results, the term probably has two origins rather than one confused internet. Adding a disambiguating Korean word to your search — 천궁 미사일 (missile) or 천궁 한약재 (medicinal herb) — fixes it immediately.
