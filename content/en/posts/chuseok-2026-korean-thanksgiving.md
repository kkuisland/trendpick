Chuseok (추석) is Korea's harvest festival and the biggest holiday of the year alongside Lunar New Year. In 2026 it falls on **Friday, September 25**, with the official holiday running **Thursday, September 24 through Saturday, September 26** — and because the Sunday after rolls straight on, most Koreans get four days off. If you are travelling to Korea that week, this is the single most important date on the calendar to plan around.

## What Chuseok actually is

Chuseok falls on the 15th day of the eighth lunar month, which is why the date moves every year. It is often translated as "Korean Thanksgiving," and the comparison works in one important way: it is the holiday where families travel home and eat together. Traditionally it is a harvest festival — a thank-you for the year's crops.

Two customs you will hear about:

- **Charye (차례)** — a memorial table set for ancestors on the morning of Chuseok, with food arranged in a specific order.
- **Seongmyo (성묘)** — visiting and tidying family graves, usually in the weeks before or on the holiday itself.

Younger families increasingly simplify or skip these rituals, and plenty of Koreans now use the break to travel instead. Both are normal.

## What people eat

The signature dish is **songpyeon (송편)**, half-moon rice cakes stuffed with sesame and honey, red bean, or chestnut, steamed over pine needles. The pine gives them a faint forest smell that is unmistakably Chuseok.

| Dish | What it is |
|---|---|
| Songpyeon (송편) | Half-moon rice cakes, sweet fillings, steamed over pine needles |
| Jeon (전) | Savoury pan-fried fritters — fish, courgette, meat patties |
| Japchae (잡채) | Sweet potato glass noodles stir-fried with vegetables and beef |
| Galbijjim (갈비찜) | Braised short ribs, the celebratory main dish |
| Toranguk (토란국) | Taro soup, a traditional Chuseok side |

If you want to try songpyeon, buy it from a bakery or market in the days *before* the holiday — many shops close during it.

## What closes, and what stays open

This is the practical part for visitors.

- **Closed or reduced**: many small restaurants, local shops, some museums, banks and government offices. Family-run places often shut for the full three days.
- **Open**: department stores and large chains usually open for at least part of the holiday, convenience stores stay open, and major tourist sites such as the palaces typically operate — several palaces have historically offered free admission during Chuseok, though this is announced each year. - **Packed**: highways, train stations and airports on the first and last days of the holiday.

Seoul itself becomes noticeably quieter during Chuseok because so many residents leave for their hometowns. Some travellers love it — the city empties out — but expect fewer options for food.

## If you're travelling to Korea that week

1. **Book transport early.** Domestic trains and buses sell out well in advance for the holiday period, and intercity roads slow to a crawl.
2. **Do not plan a big shopping day for September 25.** Plan it around the holiday instead.
3. **Have a backup restaurant list.** Your first choice may be closed; chains and hotel restaurants are the safe fallback.
4. **Expect crowds at palaces and folk villages.** Chuseok programming draws locals as well as tourists.

On accommodation: Seoul hotels are usually *easier* during Chuseok than you'd expect, because the holiday crowd is heading out of the city rather than into it. The squeeze is on domestic travel — Busan, Jeju and resort areas. If you're basing yourself in Seoul, book somewhere on a subway line and you can ignore the traffic entirely.

## Greeting people during Chuseok

The standard greeting is **"즐거운 한가위 되세요"** (jeulgeoun hangawi doeseyo) — "have a joyful Chuseok." A simpler option that always works: **"추석 잘 보내세요"** (chuseok jal bonaeseyo), roughly "spend Chuseok well."

Q: When is Chuseok 2026?
A: Chuseok day is Friday, September 25, 2026. The official public holiday runs Thursday, September 24 through Saturday, September 26, and because Sunday follows immediately, most people take four consecutive days off.

Q: Is Chuseok the same as Chinese Mid-Autumn Festival?
A: They fall on the same lunar date — the 15th day of the eighth lunar month — and both are harvest festivals, but the food, rituals and customs are distinct. Korea eats songpyeon rather than mooncakes.

Q: Will shops and restaurants be closed in Seoul?
A: Many small and family-run businesses close for the holiday. Convenience stores, large chains and most department stores stay open, at least partially. Plan your meals with a backup option.

Q: Is it a good or bad time to visit Korea?
A: It is a mixed bag. Seoul is quieter and the weather in late September is excellent, but transport is heavily booked, some businesses close, and popular cultural sites are crowded. Travelling within Korea on the first or last day of the holiday is the one thing to genuinely avoid.

Q: Why does the date change every year?
A: Chuseok follows the lunar calendar, falling on the 15th day of the eighth lunar month, so its position in the Gregorian calendar shifts each year — usually somewhere between mid-September and early October.
