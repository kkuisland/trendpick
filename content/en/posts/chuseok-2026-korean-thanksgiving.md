Chuseok (추석) is Korea's harvest festival, held on the 15th day of the eighth lunar month. In 2026 it fell on **Friday, September 25**, with the official holiday running **Thursday, September 24 through Saturday, September 26**. The next one is **Wednesday, September 15, 2027**, with the holiday running **Tuesday, September 14 through Thursday, September 16**, according to the official 2027 calendar published by the Korea AeroSpace Administration. If you are travelling to Korea in September or early October, check where Chuseok lands that year first.

## What Chuseok actually is

Chuseok falls on the 15th day of the eighth lunar month, which is why its Gregorian date moves every year. It is also called **Hangawi** (한가위). It is often translated as "Korean Thanksgiving," and the comparison works in one important way: it is the holiday when families gather and eat together. Traditionally it celebrates the harvest, and many people still travel to their hometowns for it.

Two customs you will hear about:

- **Charye (차례)** — a memorial service for ancestors held on the morning of Chuseok. Offerings include rice and rice wine made from the new harvest and songpyeon, and afterwards the family eats the food together. The exact dishes and layout differ by region and by family.
- **Seongmyo (성묘)** — visiting ancestral graves. Families clear the weeds around the graves (beolcho, 벌초) and pay their respects. Traditionally this happens after breakfast on Chuseok, with the weeding done beforehand; many families now visit the graves ahead of the holiday and hold only charye at home on the day.

## What people eat

The signature dish is **songpyeon (송편)**: small rice cakes filled with sesame seeds, beans, red beans or chestnuts, and steamed over pine needles, which give them their pine scent.

| Dish | What it is |
|---|---|
| Songpyeon (송편) | Half-moon rice cakes with sweet or nutty fillings, steamed over pine needles |
| Jeon (전) | Meat, vegetables or seafood coated in batter and egg, then pan-fried |
| Japchae (잡채) | Glass noodles stir-fried with assorted vegetables |
| Galbijjim (갈비찜) | Short ribs braised in a sweet soy-based sauce |
| Toranguk (토란국) | Taro soup, one of the traditional seasonal foods of Chuseok |

## What closes, and what stays open

This is the practical part for visitors. The Korea Tourism Organization's 2026 holiday guide describes the pattern like this:

- **Closed or reduced**: many department stores, shopping malls and restaurants close on Chuseok Day itself or for part of the holiday. National museums are generally closed on Chuseok Day but open the rest of the holiday.
- **Open**: convenience stores and shops near tourist attractions often stay open. Subways and city buses run as usual.
- **Free entry**: in 2026, Seoul's four main royal palaces, Jongmyo Shrine and the Joseon royal tombs offered free admission over the Chuseok holiday (September 24–27). Free admission is announced separately each year, so check the Korea Heritage Service notice before you go.
- **Packed**: intercity trains and express buses. Tickets for the holiday sell out quickly.

## If you're travelling to Korea during Chuseok

1. **Book intercity transport early.** Train and express bus tickets sell out fast; check when advance sales open for the route you need, and have a backup plan in case you miss out.
2. **Do not plan a big shopping day for Chuseok Day itself.** Many department stores and malls close that day.
3. **Have a backup restaurant list.** Your first choice may be closed; convenience stores and places near tourist sites are the safe fallback.
4. **Use public transport within cities.** Subways and buses keep running through the holiday.

## Greeting people during Chuseok

The standard greeting is **"즐거운 한가위 되세요"** (jeulgeoun hangawi doeseyo) — "have a joyful Chuseok." A simpler option that always works: **"추석 잘 보내세요"** (chuseok jal bonaeseyo), roughly "spend Chuseok well."
