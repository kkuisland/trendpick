Korean restaurant menus are often organised by cooking method rather than by main ingredient, which is why a first visit can feel confusing even when there's an English menu. The short version: **grilled meat (gui), stews (jjigae), rice bowls (bap), and noodles (myeon)** cover most of what you'll see, side dishes (banchan) arrive free and get refilled, and only about half of Korean food is actually spicy. This guide covers what to order, what to expect on the table, and the handful of dishes worth going out of your way for.

## The dishes worth knowing

| Dish | What it is | Spicy? |
|---|---|---|
| Samgyeopsal (삼겹살) | Thick pork belly you grill at your table | No |
| Bulgogi (불고기) | Sweet marinated beef, grilled or pan-cooked | No |
| Bibimbap (비빔밥) | Rice with vegetables, egg and chilli paste, mixed at the table | Mild, adjustable |
| Kimchi jjigae (김치찌개) | Sour-spicy stew of aged kimchi and pork | Yes |
| Sundubu jjigae (순두부찌개) | Silken tofu stew, usually with seafood | Usually |
| Tteokbokki (떡볶이) | Chewy rice cakes in sweet chilli sauce | Yes |
| Naengmyeon (냉면) | Cold buckwheat noodles in icy broth | No |
| Samgyetang (삼계탕) | Whole young chicken stuffed with rice and ginseng | No |
| Jjajangmyeon (짜장면) | Noodles in black bean sauce — Korean-Chinese, hugely popular | No |
| Korean fried chicken | Twice-fried, sauced or plain | Both exist |

If you only have a few meals: **samgyeopsal** for the experience, **kimchi jjigae** for the flavour that defines the cuisine, and **naengmyeon** if it's summer.

## Is Korean food actually spicy?

Less uniformly than its reputation suggests. Korean heat comes mostly from **gochugaru** (chilli flakes) and **gochujang** (fermented chilli paste), and it tends to be warm and slightly sweet rather than sharp. Plenty of staples — bulgogi, japchae, samgyetang, jjajangmyeon, most grilled meat — contain no chilli at all.

Useful phrase: **"안 맵게 해주세요"** (an maepge haejuseyo) — "please make it not spicy." It works at many places, though dishes built on chilli paste can't really be de-spiced.

## The free side dishes are not a mistake

Small plates called **banchan (반찬)** arrive before your food — kimchi, seasoned bean sprouts, pickled radish, braised potatoes, whatever the kitchen made that day. They are included in the price and **refills are free**. Raise a hand and ask for more of the one you liked; nobody will find it strange. Leaving them untouched is completely fine too.

Rice and soup usually also come with the meal at traditional restaurants.

## How Korean BBQ actually works

At a grill restaurant you cook the meat yourself at your table — this trips up a lot of first-timers.

1. **Order by weight, for two minimum.** Most meat is sold in 150–200g portions and most places require two orders.
2. **Let the staff help.** At many restaurants they'll cut the meat with scissors and flip it for you. It's normal to let them.
3. **Wait for the char.** Pork belly needs to be cooked through; don't rush it off the grill.
4. **Build a ssam.** Take a lettuce leaf, add meat, a dab of **ssamjang** (soybean-chilli paste), garlic or grilled kimchi, wrap it, eat in one bite. One bite is the convention.
5. **Finish with rice.** Many places will fry rice in the leftover pan juices for a small extra charge — order it.

## Table manners that matter

- **Pouring drinks**: you pour for others, not yourself. When someone older pours for you, hold your glass with both hands.
- **Chopsticks**: never stand them upright in rice — it resembles a funeral rite.
- **Shared dishes**: stews are commonly eaten communally from the centre pot. If that's uncomfortable, ask for a small bowl — **"앞접시 주세요"** (apjeopsi juseyo).
- **Tipping**: not customary in Korea. Don't leave money on the table.

## Ordering without Korean

Most restaurants in tourist areas have picture menus or English menus, and many now use tablet ordering with photos. Three phrases carry you a long way:

- **"이거 주세요"** (igeo juseyo) — "this one, please" (while pointing)
- **"계산해 주세요"** (gyesanhae juseyo) — "the bill, please"
- **"물 주세요"** (mul juseyo) — "water, please" — though water is usually self-serve from a dispenser

At many places you pay at the counter on the way out, not at the table.

## Convenience store food is genuinely good

Korean convenience stores (CU, GS25, 7-Eleven, Emart24) are a legitimate meal option, not a last resort. Triangle kimbap, instant noodles with hot water and a seating counter provided in-store, and a rotating lineup of collaboration snacks. It's how a lot of Koreans eat lunch, and it costs a fraction of a restaurant meal.

Q: What should I eat on my first day in Korea?
A: Korean BBQ (samgyeopsal) for the full experience, or kimchi jjigae if you want the single dish that best represents everyday Korean home cooking. Both are widely available and hard to get wrong.

Q: Is all Korean food spicy?
A: No. Roughly half of common Korean dishes contain no chilli — bulgogi, japchae, samgyetang, jjajangmyeon and most grilled meats are not spicy. Korean heat also tends to be warm and slightly sweet rather than sharp.

Q: Are the side dishes free?
A: Yes. Banchan are included with your meal and refills are free at most restaurants. Just ask.

Q: Do I tip in Korean restaurants?
A: No. Tipping is not customary in Korea and can cause confusion. The price on the menu is what you pay.

Q: Can vegetarians eat well in Korea?
A: It takes some planning. Many seemingly vegetable dishes use anchovy or beef stock, and kimchi often contains fermented seafood. Temple cuisine (사찰음식), bibimbap ordered without meat and egg, and the growing number of vegan restaurants in Seoul are the reliable options.

Q: What is the one dish tourists most often miss?
A: Cold noodles — naengmyeon in summer, especially the mul (broth) version. It's a genuinely distinctive dish that rarely makes it onto first-timer lists.
