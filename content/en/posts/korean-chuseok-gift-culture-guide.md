Walk into any Korean department store in the weeks before Chuseok or Seollal (Lunar New Year) and you'll find entire floors given over to elaborately boxed "gift sets" (선물세트), and some of the most popular boxes are stacked with tins of Spam. It looks strange to a first-time visitor: in the West, Spam is a pantry joke, but in Korea it's wrapped like a jewelry box and given to bosses, in-laws and clients. Gift sets are one of the biggest retail categories tied to both holidays. Here's why Spam became a status gift, what a typical gift set actually costs, and how to give one without a misstep.

## Why Spam became a Chuseok status symbol

Spam arrived on the Korean peninsula with U.S. troops during the Korean War (1950–53), at a time when meat of any kind was scarce for most civilian families. Cans that made their way off American bases through the black market were one of the few ways ordinary Koreans could get animal protein, and Uijeongbu, a city north of Seoul with many U.S. bases, became especially known for this. That same wartime scarcity economy is why *budae jjigae* (부대찌개, "army base stew"), built around Spam and other base-supply ingredients, is now a comfort-food classic.

Because it was imported, rare and protein-rich, Spam picked up a luxury association that never really wore off, even as Korea industrialized. Hormel licensed the brand to CJ CheilJedang (CJ제일제당) in 1986, and Korean-made cans began shipping in 1987. CJ says its version uses a low-temperature aging process to cut the salt and leaves out starch, so the Spam Koreans buy is a locally made product, not an import. Korea is the world's second-largest Spam market after the United States, and according to CJ, about 60% of the brand's annual Korean sales come from Chuseok and Seollal gift sets.

## What's actually inside a Korean Chuseok gift set

"Gift set" is a real, dedicated retail category in Korea, not a loose description. Department stores, big-box marts and even convenience stores pre-assemble and merchandise their own lines of holiday gift sets for Chuseok and Seollal, usually with a pre-order period that opens weeks before the holiday.

| Tier | Typical price (KRW) | What's inside |
|---|---|---|
| Budget / value | ₩30,000 – 100,000 | Tea or coffee sets, toothbrush-and-toothpaste sets, small canned-goods (Spam) boxes |
| Mid-range | ₩100,000 – 500,000 | Larger Spam or canned-goods sets, toiletry sets, fruit boxes, entry-level health-supplement sets |
| Premium | ₩500,000 and up | Hanwoo (한우, Korean beef) sets, rare seafood, aged red ginseng, flagship department-store boxes that can run into the millions of won |

The market is sharply polarized by store type. In pre-orders for Chuseok 2026 (through September 8), sets under ₩50,000 made up 77.4% of sales at E-mart and 57.5% at Lotte Mart, while at department stores, sets of ₩500,000 or more took 44.7% of sales at Shinsegae and 37.8% at Hyundai, according to the Segye Ilbo. High-end items such as Hanwoo and wine drive department-store sales, while marts lean on value sets.

## Chuseok gift-giving etiquette: who gives what to whom

- **Visiting family for the holiday?** Bring something. A gift set is the standard thing to carry when visiting parents' or in-laws' homes for Chuseok or Seollal.
- **Health and longevity gifts** (red ginseng, vitamins, general health-supplement sets) are the classic choice for parents, grandparents and parents-in-law.
- **Giving to a boss or senior colleague?** Stick to practical, "can't-go-wrong" items such as food gift sets or health-related sets.
- **Company-to-client or company-to-partner gifts** tend to be wine, quality food sets or gift cards, which are treated as safe, professional choices.
- **Presentation matters.** Gifts are given and received with both hands (or the right hand supported by the left), with a slight bow, deeper for someone clearly senior. Red or yellow wrapping reads as auspicious; white or black wrapping is avoided because of its funeral association.

Gift-giving directly between coworkers of the same rank is less standard and can create a sense of reciprocal pressure, unless the coworkers are already close. Most holiday gifting in Korea flows either within family or up and down a hierarchy (junior to senior, company to client), not sideways between equals.

## Korea's legal cap on holiday gift value

This is the detail that surprises most foreign readers: Korea has a statutory limit on gift value for public officials, journalists and private-school staff, under the **Improper Solicitation and Graft Act** (청탁금지법, widely known as the "Kim Young-ran Act"). The law even carves out a special, higher limit for Chuseok and Seollal to support domestic farmers and fishers, whose fruit, beef and seafood are the traditional core of holiday gift-giving.

| Gift category | Normal cap | Chuseok / Seollal gift period |
|---|---|---|
| General gifts to covered officials | ₩50,000 | No change |
| Agricultural, livestock and fishery products (and goods made from them) | ₩150,000 | ₩300,000 |
| Meals with covered officials | ₩50,000 | No change |

The agricultural and fishery limits were last raised in August 2023, from ₩100,000 (₩200,000 during the holidays). Separately, the cap on meals with officials rose from ₩30,000 to ₩50,000 in August 2024. The higher holiday limit applies from 24 days before Seollal or Chuseok to 5 days after. Korea's Anti-Corruption and Civil Rights Commission reviews the caps periodically.

The law only covers gifts to specific professions (public servants, teachers, journalists), not gift-giving between ordinary families or private companies. That side of holiday gifting has no legal ceiling at all, which is part of why department-store flagship sets can run into the millions of won.

## How Korean holiday gifting differs from Western gifting

Western holiday gifts, especially at Christmas, tend to be personal and individually chosen, something specific to the recipient. Chuseok and Seollal gifting runs the opposite way: the ideal gift is practical and shareable by an entire household (food staples, toiletries, cooking oil, canned goods), because the point of the gesture is reciprocity and respect between families or within a hierarchy, not a personalized surprise.

That's also the whole explanation for the Spam paradox. An everyday pantry item in the U.S. becomes gift-worthy in Korea through its history, premium boxing, matched sets and placement on a department-store gift floor.
